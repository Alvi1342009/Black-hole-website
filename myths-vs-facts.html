🔍 What Do We Really Know About Black Holes?

Black holes can seem mysterious and frightening because we cannot see them directly. Movies, social media, and popular stories have also created many myths about them. Here, we'll compare some common ideas with what scientists actually understand about black holes.

❌

MYTH

Black holes suck up everything around them.

A black hole does not act like a giant cosmic vacuum cleaner.

✅

FACT

Black holes have gravity, just like other massive objects.

An object must come sufficiently close to a black hole to be captured by its gravity. If our Sun were magically replaced by a black hole with the same mass, the planets would continue orbiting in almost the same way.

❌

MYTH

Black holes are giant holes in space.

They are not empty holes or tunnels floating through space.

✅

FACT

Black holes are regions of extremely strong gravity.

They form when a large amount of matter becomes compressed into an extremely small region. Their gravity becomes so strong that, beyond the event horizon, even light cannot escape.

❌

MYTH

Every black hole is enormous.

All black holes are not the same size.

✅

FACT

Black holes come in different sizes.

Scientists know about stellar-mass black holes, intermediate-mass candidates, and enormous supermassive black holes containing millions or billions of times the mass of the Sun.

❌

MYTH

You are instantly crushed the moment you cross the event horizon.

The experience depends strongly on the size of the black hole.

✅

FACT

The event horizon is a boundary, not a physical wall.

For a sufficiently massive black hole, an astronaut could cross the event horizon without immediately noticing anything special at that exact boundary. However, the extreme gravitational environment deeper inside can eventually become fatal.

❌

MYTH

Black holes travel around space looking for things to eat.

They are not cosmic predators chasing stars and planets.

✅

FACT

Black holes follow gravity and the motion of their surroundings.

Like other massive objects, black holes can orbit stars, move through galaxies, and interact gravitationally with other objects.
